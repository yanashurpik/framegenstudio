AI UGC ad generator vs. an AI UGC studio: what $5 tools don't get you

Self-serve AI UGC tools advertise $3-10 per video. That's the cost of one generation — not the cost of one ad you'd actually run. Getting there usually takes several attempts, and the real cost is what happens between the headline price and a finished, ad-ready video.

By Yana Shurpik Jul 2026 7 min read

The headline price is the cost of one attempt, not one ad

$3-10 per video sounds like a rounding error next to a $3,000 influencer shoot, and on paper it is. But that number describes a single generation — one pass through the model. It doesn't describe what it takes to get a version that's actually ready to publish: consistent character across the clip, lip sync that holds up, a hook that isn't generic, footage that doesn't look obviously synthetic.

Generative video is a probabilistic process. The first output is rarely the one you'd run as a paid ad — that's true of every model in this category, not a flaw specific to one tool. The difference between a self-serve tool and a studio isn't whether iteration happens. It's who absorbs the cost of that iteration, and whether the process that gets you to a usable result is built in or left entirely to you.

What the reviews actually say

Independent review data on the major self-serve AI UGC tools tells a consistent story:

  • Arcads holds a 2.8 Trustpilot score across roughly 142 reviews, with reported issues including glitchy, out-of-sync lip syncing and unresponsive support.
  • HeyGen holds a 2.3 Trustpilot score. Output is typically a polished avatar clip that still needs captions, B-roll, and a finishing pass in a separate tool before it's ad-ready.
  • Creatify's fast-draft output is commonly described as needing manual cleanup before it can run as a paid ad.

None of this means these tools are broken — they're built for a different job: fast, high-volume drafts for someone who already has the skill and time to iterate, clean up, and assemble the finished ad themselves. The gap between "a video came out" and "an ad is ready to run" is real, and it's where most of the actual cost hides.

The real math, once you add it up

The total cost of a self-serve ad isn't the credit price. It's:

  • Credits spent on regenerations until character consistency, lip sync, and delivery land — often several attempts per usable clip.
  • Your time writing and testing hooks, since the tool generates footage, not a tested angle for your specific product and audience.
  • A cleanup or finishing pass in a separate editor, since raw output frequently isn't ad-ready as-is.
  • The eventual professional spend, if the iteration takes long enough that you go looking for an agency anyway — at which point the time and credits already spent were effectively sunk.

None of this shows up in the $3-10 headline number. It's not that the tools are lying about price — it's that the price describes the smallest possible unit of the job, not the job itself.
